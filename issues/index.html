ISSUES

Budgets, not speeches.

Here's how I look at some of the work facing Gardner. These are my views, not a platform for an office I haven't decided to seek.

STRAIGHT ANSWERS.
REAL TRADE-OFFS.

BudgetInfrastructureSafetySchoolsHousingBusinessCity Hall

I've spent a lot of time with municipal budgets. Most public debates start with what we'd like to do. The harder part is finding the money, understanding what else it displaces, and making sure the thing actually gets done.

That doesn't mean saying no to everything. It means being clear about what we're buying and why.

01

Taxes and the city budget

People work hard for what they earn. They deserve to know what the city is spending, what's driving costs up, and which expenses are being pushed into another year.

Budgets should show the real trade-offs instead of hiding behind vague language. If a department needs more people, equipment, or money, we should explain the need. If something has to wait, explain that too. A tax bill doesn't become easier to pay because City Hall uses nicer words.

02

Roads, buildings, water, and infrastructure

We can't keep treating maintenance like a surprise. Streets, water systems, public buildings, and vehicles wear out whether or not we plan for them.

A real capital plan should say what we own, what condition it's in, what the work will cost, and the order in which it needs to happen. Some things will always break unexpectedly. That doesn't excuse failing to plan for the things we already know are coming.

03

Police, fire, EMS, and emergency communications

Public safety is people, equipment, training, buildings, and the systems behind them. I've worked with public safety departments and on regional emergency communications. I know that a good response depends on much more than what happens when someone calls 911.

We need to be serious about staffing, recruitment, facilities, and the cost of keeping reliable service. Regional cooperation can help when it's done well, but it shouldn't become an excuse to make local accountability disappear.

04

Schools and families

School costs are a major part of any municipal budget, and families need stable programs they can count on. The city can't pretend schools and municipal services are separate conversations when residents pay for both.

State aid, transportation, facilities, staffing, and enrollment all affect the bill. I'd rather talk about those numbers plainly than argue from slogans.

05

Housing and neighborhoods

Working families, young people, and seniors should have a reasonable chance to stay in Gardner. Housing costs are making that harder.

But a housing plan also needs to account for roads, water, schools, and the character of the neighborhoods where new homes are proposed. Growth has to be practical. The people who already live here deserve a say, and the people trying to make a life here deserve a fair chance.

06

Jobs and local business

City Hall can't manufacture a successful business, but it can make starting or expanding one easier—or needlessly harder. Permitting, clear rules, utility costs, and predictable decisions matter.

Local employers need answers they can plan around. We should be straightforward about what a project requires, what the timeline looks like, and who is responsible for the next step.

07

A City Hall that answers people

Constituent service isn't an add-on to government. It's the job. People should be able to find the right person, get a direct answer, and understand what happens next.

I've worked on municipal records, procedures, and digital systems because they affect whether anyone can find an answer six months later. Technology should make local government easier to use, not put another screen between a resident and someone who can help.
