01 / WHY NOW

Local roots.
Real experience.

My grandparents raised me in Gardner with a simple idea: take care of the place that takes care of you.

I spent five terms representing Ward 3 on the Gardner City Council. That meant budgets and public meetings, and it also meant neighborhood concerns, basic services, and the follow-through people should be able to expect from their city.

Then I went to work on the administrative side of local government. I was Town Clerk in Princeton and Town Administrator in Hubbardston, where I managed an $11 million budget, negotiated union contracts, brought in more than $2 million in grants and saw a new municipal and public safety complex through. Before that I was chief of staff in the Marlborough mayor's office and in the State Senate. I've seen how a mayor's office runs from the inside, and how decisions on Beacon Hill land on a city's budget.

The work I'm proudest of keeps working after I leave. When the shared dispatch setup five towns relied on stopped serving them, I helped build a regional 911 district so their calls get answered.

Gardner's mayor's office changes hands in January. Before I decide whether to run, and for which office, I want to understand what Gardner needs next and whether I'm the right person to help.

03 / THE BUDGET TEST

Five questions.
Every budget.

These are the questions I ask of any budget. Gardner's should be able to answer all five in public.

  1. 1

    Does it add up?

    Every budget order matches the spreadsheet behind it, and every account reconciles.

  2. 2

    Who decided?

    Spending follows the charter, the ordinances and the votes that allow it.

  3. 3

    What does it buy?

    Plowed streets, paved roads, schools that work, calls that get answered.

  4. 4

    Can you follow it?

    Open hearings, plain explanations, and numbers posted where people can find them.

  5. 5

    Will it last?

    Written procedures and shared systems, so the city never depends on one person's memory.

Reading Gardner's FY2027 budget

Mayor Nicholson and the City Council put real work into this budget. Here's what I'd build on and where I'd push further. The number on each item is the question it answers.

Build on

  • Question 4, can you follow it: The Council's new process: committee-of-the-whole reviews, an early deadline for amendments, and room for the mayor to adjust before the final votes.43
  • Question 1, does it add up: Council review cut more than $2 million from where the process started.4
  • Question 3, what does it buy: A proposed $1 million cut to the school budget was voted down.4

Push further

  • Question 1, does it add up: Generate the budget orders directly from the budget spreadsheet. The June review found rounding and transposition errors that had to be corrected and resubmitted.3
  • Question 3, what does it buy: Budget snow and ice at what winters actually cost, and say so in public. Councilors questioned whether this year's line was enough.3
  • Question 4, can you follow it: Publish a line-by-line schedule for professional development and travel, which this year moved into one consolidated account.35
  • Question 1, does it add up: Plan health insurance at its real trend. The Finance Committee was looking at a 12.5% Blue Cross renewal.5
  • Question 5, will it last: Write down how the city's money work gets done, step by step, so retirements and turnover don't set Gardner back.

Gardner on Beacon Hill

A lot of what City Hall can do runs through the Legislature: charter changes, land votes and the aid formulas.

  • Chapter 406 of the Acts of 2024 · Signed January 10, 2025

    Gardner's charter, updated

    Rewrote major parts of the city charter. The mayor's term stays at two years.8

  • Chapter 137 of the Acts of 2024

    Waterford Street school

    Lets the city use the former Waterford Street school building and land for general municipal purposes.9

  • FY2027 state budget · Signed July 9, 2026

    The last year of the Student Opportunity Act

    Funds the final year of the law's schedule and directs a commission to study the Chapter 70 school aid formula.67

  • My own work · 2026

    Fixing the Chapter 70 formula

    I went through the Chapter 70 formula line by line and wrote a reform package for districts like ours, which are losing enrollment while costs keep rising. I sent it to the Foundation Budget Review Commission and the Joint Committee on Education.10

Gardner's new State Representative knows City Hall. Whoever leads the city next should work with him on the home-rule petitions and aid formulas that decide what Gardner can do.

05 / THE QUESTIONS

Let's talk about
what matters.

These aren't campaign promises or a finished platform. They're the questions I believe Gardner's leaders should be ready to answer.

  1. Taxes & the budget

    What are the real trade-offs? What can the city afford, and can residents follow the money?

  2. Roads & infrastructure

    Are we maintaining what we have, and planning projects before repairs become emergencies?

  3. Public safety

    Do police, fire, EMS and dispatch have the staff, equipment and coordination to serve well?

  4. Housing & neighborhoods

    How can families and seniors find homes they can afford, with development that matches what our roads, water and schools can handle?

  5. Jobs & local business

    Can city services, permitting and planning make Gardner a practical place to start and grow a business?

  6. A City Hall that works

    Can people get straight answers, follow decisions and resolve issues without being sent in circles?
